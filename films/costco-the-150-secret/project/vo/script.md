## 01
One dollar and fifty cents.
In 1985, that could buy you a hot dog and a soda at Costco.
More than forty years later, the price is still one dollar and fifty cents.
Think about everything that has changed. Rent. Wages. Food costs. The price of almost everything around us.
Yet one of America's largest retailers keeps selling this combination for the same price.
Why?
The answer is bigger than a hot dog.
## 02
Keeping a price unchanged for four decades is unusual.
Inflation means the same dollar buys less over time. Businesses normally respond by raising prices, reducing costs, or changing what they sell.
Costco chose a different path for its most recognizable food-court item.
The company protected the price while adapting the operation behind it.
And that raises a more interesting question.
What makes a cheap hot dog valuable to a retailer selling hundreds of billions of dollars in merchandise?
## 03
To understand the hot dog, you first have to understand Costco.
Costco isn't designed like a conventional supermarket.
Its warehouses are enormous. Products are frequently sold in bulk.
The shelves are practical, the presentation is restrained, and the assortment is deliberately limited.
The company wants to sell large volumes of merchandise while keeping operating costs under control.
Instead of convincing customers to pay more for an elaborate shopping experience, Costco works to convince them they're getting more for their money.
## 04
There's another difference.
Most shoppers pay Costco before they buy their groceries.
They purchase an annual membership.
That changes the relationship between retailer and customer.
Costco must give people a reason to join, and then a reason to renew.
Every visit becomes an opportunity to demonstrate value.
Low prices aren't simply a promotional message. They're part of the promise customers expect their membership to deliver.
And that promise is central to the business.
## 05
In fiscal 2025, Costco reported more than five point three billion dollars in membership fee revenue.
Its renewal rate in the United States and Canada was over ninety-two percent.
Those figures tell us something important.
Costco has built a business where customers repeatedly pay for the privilege of shopping there.
That revenue supports the overall economics, although it doesn't mean every membership dollar is pure profit.
The retailer has a powerful incentive to protect the value customers believe they're receiving.
## 06
Now look at the shelves.
Costco doesn't try to offer every possible version of every product.
By concentrating demand on a smaller assortment, it can purchase greater volumes of individual items.
That can strengthen negotiating power, simplify inventory and reduce handling costs.
The important lesson is that low prices aren't created at the checkout.
They're created through thousands of decisions made before a product reaches the shelf.
## 07
But the hot dog presents a special challenge.
Unlike a television or a box of detergent, it has to be prepared and served.
There are ingredients, equipment, labor, packaging and drinks to consider.
And over time, those costs change.
Costco could have responded by increasing the price.
Instead, it focused on controlling the operation behind the meal.
The question became less about how much more customers would pay, and more about how efficiently Costco could keep serving them.
## 08
Costco has changed parts of the hot dog operation while keeping the headline price.
It moved toward its own Kirkland Signature hot dogs, giving the company more control over sourcing and production.
Its purchasing scale also matters.
Stronger buying power helps Costco keep costs down.
None of this means the combo is immune to inflation.
It means the company has repeatedly chosen to defend the customer-facing price by working on the economics behind it.
## 09
Here's where the story becomes more interesting.
Customers cannot memorize the price of every product in a warehouse.
But they can remember one dollar and fifty cents.
That makes the hot dog an unusually visible symbol of Costco's pricing philosophy.
Every time a shopper sees that familiar price, the company reinforces a simple message.
We're still trying to give you value.
The hot dog doesn't prove every item is the cheapest.
It makes the promise of value easier to recognize.
## 10
Think about what happens when a company keeps a familiar promise for decades.
Customers begin to recognize a pattern.
They may come to expect consistency, especially from a retailer whose business depends on repeat visits and membership renewals.
The hot dog is one highly visible example of that consistency.
But there's an important distinction.
Costco's membership performance is measurable.
The exact contribution of the hot dog to renewals is not publicly established.
The connection is a strategic interpretation, not a proven dollar-for-dollar return.
## 11
The hot dog is a tiny part of an enormous retail operation.
In fiscal 2025, Costco reported nearly two hundred and seventy billion dollars in net sales.
Its model depends on moving large quantities of merchandise through a disciplined warehouse network.
Scale can strengthen purchasing power, while operational simplicity can help limit costs.
That doesn't make every product equally profitable.
It does mean Costco evaluates its business as a system, not simply as a collection of isolated price tags.
## 12
So why doesn't every retailer simply copy the one-dollar-fifty-cent hot dog?
Because copying a price is easy.
Copying the system supporting it is much harder.
A competitor would need its own purchasing advantages, efficient operations, customer relationships and reasons for shoppers to return.
Discounting one item without those advantages may simply reduce earnings.
Costco's real strength isn't that it discovered a magical price.
It's that the price fits within a broader business model built to deliver value consistently.
## 13
There's another detail that makes the hot dog decision revealing.
Costco does raise prices when its broader economics require it.
In September 2024, it increased annual membership fees in the United States and Canada.
Yet the famous hot dog combo remained at one dollar and fifty cents.
That contrast matters.
The company is not committed to freezing every price forever.
It makes different decisions about different parts of the business.
The hot dog appears to occupy a special place in how Costco communicates value.
## 14
It's tempting to call the hot dog a loss leader and leave the story there.
But that explanation skips an important question.
Does Costco actually lose money on every combo?
The company's public financial reports don't give us a precise answer.
We can document the price. We can examine its supply chain decisions. And we can measure Costco's broader financial performance.
What we cannot responsibly do is invent a per-hot-dog loss and present it as fact.
The real strategy is interesting enough without that claim.
## 15
The most valuable part of this story may not be the hot dog itself.
It may be the expectation the price helps reinforce.
A customer enters Costco believing the company is serious about value.
The membership model gives Costco a reason to keep earning that belief.
And its operating system is designed to support competitive pricing at scale.
The hot dog is a symbol of those choices.
Small enough to remember. Familiar enough to notice. And unusual enough to make people ask why.
## 16
So why does Costco refuse to raise the price of its famous hot dog?
Because the decision represents something larger than the meal.
It demonstrates a commitment to recognizable value, supported by purchasing scale, operating discipline and a membership business that rewards customer loyalty.
Costco hasn't frozen the entire business in 1985.
It has adapted the business while protecting one extraordinary price.
Sometimes the clearest evidence of a company's strategy isn't found in a boardroom.
It's printed above a hot dog.
Every brand leaves evidence.
