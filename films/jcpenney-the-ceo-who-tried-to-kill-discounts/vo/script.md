# SCENE 01
In 2012, one of America's oldest department store chains attempted something extraordinary.

It tried to break its customers' addiction to discounts.

No endless coupons. No confusing markdowns. No constant promotional theater.

Instead, JCPenney would offer simpler, more predictable prices.

The idea came from an executive who had helped transform retail at Apple.

But within one fiscal year, JCPenney's annual sales had fallen by more than four billion dollars.

How could a strategy designed to make shopping easier produce such a devastating result?

# SCENE 02
Ron Johnson arrived at JCPenney with an extraordinary reputation.

He had led Apple's retail operations, helping establish a store experience that became one of the most admired in the industry.

Before Apple, he had worked at Target.

In November 2011, he became JCPenney's chief executive.

The company was struggling to remain relevant.

Johnson wasn't hired to preserve the status quo.

He was hired to transform it.

And his first major target was something deeply embedded in the department store business.

The way merchandise was priced.

# SCENE 03
JCPenney had been part of American retail since 1902.

Generations of shoppers knew its department stores, clothing, household goods, and familiar promotional events.

But by the early twenty-first century, traditional department stores faced growing pressure.

Competition was intensifying.

Customers had more places to shop.

And JCPenney had become heavily dependent on promotional activity.

The company needed a reason for shoppers to choose it.

Johnson believed the answer was not another coupon.

It was a completely different shopping experience.

# SCENE 04
In 2011, JCPenney ran more than five hundred and ninety promotional events.

For customers, that meant an almost constant stream of sales, offers, and markdowns.

But for management, the system created a different problem.

If merchandise was always on sale, what did the original price actually mean?

Johnson believed customers would welcome a more straightforward approach.

Instead of navigating a complicated promotional calendar, they could shop with confidence.

There was one crucial assumption behind that idea.

That customers valued a simpler price more than the feeling of getting a bargain.

# SCENE 05
On January twenty-fifth, 2012, JCPenney unveiled its transformation plan.

The new pricing system was called Fair and Square.

It introduced three categories.

Everyday prices.

Month-long values.

And the company's lowest prices, offered at designated times.

The new strategy began on February first.

JCPenney was not eliminating every promotion.

But it was abandoning the constant cycle of coupons and temporary markdowns that had defined its relationship with many customers.

The company was changing more than prices.

It was changing how shoppers understood value.

# SCENE 06
On paper, the logic was compelling.

Simpler prices could reduce confusion.

Customers wouldn't need to wait for a coupon or wonder whether a sale was genuine.

The business could spend less energy creating promotional events and more energy improving its merchandise and stores.

Johnson also envisioned a new retail environment built around individual branded shops.

The ambition was enormous.

But a successful strategy requires more than internal logic.

It must fit the behavior of the people who actually buy the product.

And JCPenney was about to discover that distinction.

# SCENE 07
Imagine two shirts.

One has a regular price of forty dollars.

The other is marked down from sixty dollars to forty.

The customer pays exactly the same amount.

But the second shirt appears to offer a bigger bargain.

That difference is a familiar feature of retail psychology.

A reference price can influence how attractive an offer feels.

JCPenney's new approach challenged that habit.

It asked shoppers to trust the everyday price without the reassurance of a dramatic markdown.

And for customers accustomed to promotions, that was a major change.

# SCENE 08
The early results were troubling.

Customers did not embrace the transformation at the scale management had anticipated.

Sales weakened sharply.

JCPenney adjusted its messaging and promotional approach as the year progressed.

But the company was simultaneously changing prices, marketing, merchandise, and the store experience.

That made the turnaround unusually difficult.

If customers stopped visiting, which part of the transformation was responsible?

The pricing?

The products?

The advertising?

Or the pace of change itself?

The financial results would soon make the problem impossible to ignore.

# SCENE 09
In fiscal 2011, JCPenney reported seventeen point two six billion dollars in net sales.

In fiscal 2012, that figure fell to twelve point nine nine billion.

A decline of approximately twenty-four point eight percent.

Comparable-store sales fell twenty-five point two percent.

The company's gross margin also weakened.

These figures did not isolate the impact of pricing from every other business change.

But they revealed the scale of the crisis.

A transformation intended to revive JCPenney had coincided with one of the most damaging years in its modern history.

# SCENE 10
The annual report described declining store traffic and lower conversion rates.

Fewer visits.

Fewer transactions.

Fewer items sold.

That was particularly dangerous for a department store chain carrying substantial fixed operating costs.

A store still requires employees, inventory, utilities, and physical space even when customers stop buying.

The new strategy had not simply failed to create the expected excitement.

The business was losing the sales volume it needed to support its operations.

And reversing that momentum would require more than another advertising campaign.

# SCENE 11
Pricing was only one part of Johnson's plan.

He wanted JCPenney stores to become collections of curated branded shops.

He pursued changes to merchandise, presentation, marketing, and the customer experience.

The strategy was ambitious because it attempted to modernize the entire retailer.

But the different initiatives were interconnected.

Changing merchandise could unsettle established shoppers.

Changing promotions could alter their reasons for visiting.

Changing store layouts required time and investment.

JCPenney was trying to rebuild its identity while still depending on its existing customers to fund the transformation.

# SCENE 12
By 2013, the pressure on JCPenney's leadership was intense.

The company had already begun modifying its promotional approach.

Its disappointing results had damaged confidence in the turnaround.

On April eighth, 2013, Ron Johnson was replaced as chief executive.

Former CEO Myron Ullman returned to lead the company.

Johnson's tenure had lasted less than eighteen months.

The executive brought in to reinvent JCPenney had departed before his broader store transformation could be completed.

The question now was whether the company could win back the customers it had lost.

# SCENE 13
JCPenney began rebuilding its promotional relationship with shoppers.

The company restored familiar marketing approaches and worked to stabilize its merchandise strategy.

But customer trust and shopping habits do not return automatically.

The retailer faced the challenge of recovering sales while dealing with the financial consequences of its failed transformation.

And the competitive pressures that existed before Johnson arrived had not disappeared.

Department stores still faced changing consumer behavior and powerful alternatives.

JCPenney had to recover from an internal strategic shock while continuing to compete in a difficult industry.

# SCENE 14
In May 2020, JCPenney filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection.

The company had struggled for years with financial pressure and changing retail conditions.

The COVID-19 pandemic added another severe disruption.

It would be misleading to claim that Ron Johnson's pricing strategy alone caused the bankruptcy seven years later.

The business faced multiple structural challenges.

But the 2012 collapse remained an important chapter in its history.

A costly transformation had weakened the company during a period when it desperately needed a sustainable path forward.

# SCENE 15
The evidence points to four strategic problems.

First, management underestimated how customers responded to discounts.

Second, it attempted to change deeply established shopping habits too quickly.

Third, the company transformed pricing, merchandise, marketing, and stores at the same time.

And fourth, the scale of the experiment created enormous financial pressure before the new model had proved itself.

Fair and Square was not necessarily a bad idea in every retail environment.

But at JCPenney, the strategy collided with the expectations of the customers the company needed most.

# SCENE 16
Ron Johnson had helped build one of the most successful retail experiences in the world.

But JCPenney was not Apple.

Its customers had different expectations.

Its business faced different constraints.

And the habits that brought shoppers through its doors could not be erased by a new pricing philosophy.

The lesson is bigger than coupons.

A company can design a strategy that looks elegant on paper and still misunderstand the people it serves.

Because changing a business is not the same as changing its customers.

And every brand leaves evidence.
