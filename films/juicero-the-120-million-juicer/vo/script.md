# Case 011 · Juicero: the $120 million juicer disaster — narration (verbatim)

## 01
On a kitchen counter sits a four-hundred-dollar machine backed by some of Silicon Valley's most prominent investors. Beside it, a sealed packet of fruit and vegetables. In April 2017, Bloomberg reporters conducted a remarkably simple experiment. Instead of using the machine, they squeezed the packet with their hands. Juice came out. A company that had raised approximately one hundred and twenty million dollars suddenly faced a devastating question. What exactly were customers paying for?
## 02
Juicero wasn't an obscure invention struggling to attract attention. Its investors included Google Ventures, Kleiner Perkins, and Campbell Soup. These were organizations with experience in technology, consumer products, and ambitious new businesses. The opportunity appeared to combine several attractive ideas. Healthy living, connected appliances, convenience, and recurring purchases. Juicero wasn't merely selling a kitchen appliance. It was attempting to build an entire commercial ecosystem around the daily habit of drinking fresh juice.
## 03
Behind the company was Doug Evans, an entrepreneur who believed fresh produce should become a more convenient part of people's lives. Traditional juicing required preparation, equipment, and cleanup. Buying prepared juice was easier, but Juicero offered another possibility. Fresh juice at home without handling loose ingredients or cleaning a conventional juicer. The underlying consumer problem was understandable. The controversial decision was how Evans and his company chose to solve it. They approached a food preparation problem as a technology platform.
## 04
In March 2016, Juicero launched its connected countertop press. The price was six hundred and ninety-nine dollars. Customers inserted proprietary packets containing prepared fruits and vegetables. The machine pressed their contents into a glass, without requiring the customer to handle the loose produce. It was designed to make juicing cleaner and more consistent. But the starting price created an immediate challenge. Before Juicero could sell millions of servings, it first had to convince customers to purchase an unusually expensive appliance.
## 05
Selling the machine was only the beginning. Juicero also supplied the refrigerated produce packets used by the press. Each purchase created another opportunity for revenue. The model resembled other appliance businesses built around compatible consumables. But fresh vegetables are different from coffee capsules or printer cartridges. They deteriorate. They require careful handling and dependable distribution. Juicero's business therefore depended on two products succeeding together. Customers needed a reason to buy the machine, and then a reason to keep buying the packets.
## 06
Juicero's press incorporated internet connectivity and a system for identifying its produce packets. The company argued that this technology helped protect freshness and improve consistency. It was trying to control the experience from delivery to consumption. But every additional feature introduced another question. Did customers value the technology enough to justify the price and dependence on a proprietary system? Juicero had created a tightly integrated product. That integration could offer benefits, but it also made the appliance dependent on the wider business.
## 07
The Juicero Press was not simply an empty shell with a motor inside. Its construction involved a sophisticated mechanical system and specialized components. A third-party teardown revealed the complexity of the engineering. From that perspective, the machine was an ambitious piece of hardware. But engineering achievement and commercial value are not the same thing. A product can be technically impressive while remaining difficult to justify economically. The more complicated the solution becomes, the more important it is to establish that customers genuinely need what it delivers.
## 08
Before the infamous experiment, Juicero had already changed its pricing strategy. In January 2017, the company reduced the price of its press from six hundred and ninety-nine dollars to three hundred and ninety-nine. That's a reduction of roughly forty-three percent. Lowering the price made the product accessible to more potential customers. But it also demonstrated that the original price was difficult to sustain as a route to wider adoption. And the company had not yet faced the public revelation that would transform its reputation.
## 09
Then came the Bloomberg investigation. Reporters compared the output of Juicero's machine with what they could extract manually from the same type of packet. The machine produced slightly more juice, but hand squeezing delivered a remarkably similar result. In the documented comparison, the manual method was also faster. The difference wasn't enough to make every benefit of the appliance disappear. But it exposed a much larger problem. The most visible function of a four-hundred-dollar machine could be substantially reproduced without buying it.
## 10
Until then, Juicero's story had been about technology improving nutrition and convenience. The investigation introduced a much simpler narrative. An expensive machine performing a task people could accomplish themselves. That contrast was easy to understand and difficult to overcome. The brand's challenge was no longer just explaining why its product was useful. It had to defend why the complete system was worth purchasing. And it had to do so while its hardware became the subject of widespread public criticism.
## 11
Juicero's leadership rejected the idea that its value could be measured solely by hand squeezing a packet. Chief executive Jeff Dunn defended the broader system, emphasizing convenience, product consistency, and quality control. The company also offered dissatisfied customers refunds. Those arguments deserve consideration. A machine can provide benefits beyond its most basic mechanical function. But Juicero now needed to demonstrate that those additional benefits justified the cost. Its defense depended on customers valuing the complete experience rather than simply the juice.
## 12
The hand-squeezing experiment became the defining image of Juicero's failure. But behind that image was another challenge. Every produce packet had to be sourced, prepared, packaged, transported, and delivered while maintaining freshness. The business was operating two demanding systems at once. Consumer hardware and fresh-food distribution. Increasing sales meant expanding that infrastructure. And reducing prices meant finding ways to deliver the same experience at lower cost. The appliance attracted attention. The distribution network represented a less visible, but equally important, business problem.
## 13
By July 2017, Juicero was pursuing another attempt to make its system more affordable. The company announced plans to reduce staffing by twenty-five percent and work toward lower prices for its appliance and produce packs. Contemporary reporting described ambitions for a press costing less than two hundred dollars. These were plans, not a successful commercial turnaround. Juicero was attempting to restructure its costs while defending a damaged product proposition. It needed cheaper hardware, cheaper distribution, and enough customers to support both.
## 14
On September first, 2017, Juicero announced that it was suspending sales of its press and produce packets. The company said it had sold more than a million produce packs since launching. But it concluded that the infrastructure necessary to serve a national customer base was beyond what it could establish independently. Juicero began seeking an acquirer with an existing fresh-food supply chain and offered refunds on its presses. Approximately seventeen months after launch, the ambitious consumer technology business could no longer continue independently.
## 15
The evidence points toward three strategic weaknesses. An expensive appliance placed a high barrier in front of customer adoption. A proprietary fresh-food ecosystem introduced significant operational demands. And the product's central function could be reproduced through a dramatically cheaper alternative. None of these observations alone explains every decision inside Juicero. Together, however, they reveal a business struggling to align engineering, customer value, and distribution economics. Venture funding created the opportunity to build the system. It couldn't guarantee that the system would become sustainable.
## 16
Juicero wasn't a failure because innovation is unnecessary. It was a reminder that innovation must justify its own complexity. Approximately one hundred and twenty million dollars in funding helped create an extraordinary machine. But customers still needed a compelling reason to buy it, and the company needed an affordable way to support it. The most important lesson wasn't revealed inside a boardroom or a financial report. It was revealed when someone picked up a packet and squeezed. Every brand leaves evidence.
