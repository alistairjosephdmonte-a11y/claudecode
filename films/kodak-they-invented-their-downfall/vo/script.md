## 1
1975. Inside a Kodak laboratory in Rochester, New York, an engineer named Steven Sasson was building something extraordinary.
It weighed about eight pounds. It captured black-and-white images. And it recorded them onto magnetic tape.
The resolution? Just ten thousand pixels.
By modern standards, it was primitive.
But the principle was revolutionary.
A camera that could take photographs without photographic film.
And the company behind it was Kodak.
## 2
Thirty-seven years later, on January nineteenth, 2012, Eastman Kodak filed for Chapter Eleven bankruptcy protection.
The company that helped pioneer digital photography had failed to build a sustainable business through the industry's transformation.
The popular explanation is simple.
Kodak invented the digital camera, ignored it, and paid the price.
But that isn't quite what happened.
Kodak didn't simply ignore digital photography.
It invested in it.
Which makes the real question far more interesting.
How did Kodak see the future and still lose its position in it?
## 3
For much of the twentieth century, Kodak was one of the most recognizable names in photography.
Its products helped millions of people capture birthdays, weddings, holidays and ordinary moments.
The company made cameras, photographic film, paper and processing equipment.
And its famous yellow packaging became part of everyday life.
But Kodak's success wasn't simply about making cameras.
Its real strength was the enormous commercial system surrounding every photograph.
A system that encouraged customers to keep buying.
## 4
Consider the economics of traditional photography.
A customer bought a camera.
Then they bought film.
After taking photographs, they paid to have that film developed and printed.
Then they bought more film.
For Kodak, photography created recurring demand for products and services long after the original camera purchase.
This was an extraordinarily attractive business structure.
But it depended on something fundamental.
People had to keep using physical film.
And digital photography threatened to remove that requirement entirely.
## 5
Sasson's prototype demonstrated that a photograph could become electronic information.
No chemical development.
No conventional film negative.
No physical print required to see the image.
But the technology was far from ready for mass adoption.
The camera was bulky, image quality was limited, and storing photographs was slow.
Kodak had time to respond.
And over the following decades, the company continued developing digital imaging technology.
The challenge wasn't simply recognizing what might become possible.
It was understanding what would happen to Kodak's business when that possibility became normal.
## 6
Imagine running a company whose most valuable business depends on customers buying something repeatedly.
Now imagine developing a technology that allows those customers to stop buying it.
That was Kodak's strategic dilemma.
Digital photography offered enormous possibilities.
But it also threatened the recurring economics of film, processing and printing.
The company needed to develop a new business while its established business remained valuable.
And the new technology didn't automatically offer the same profitability.
Kodak had to manage two very different economic worlds at once.
## 7
Here's the part of the story that often gets overlooked.
Kodak did not abandon digital photography.
In 1991, it introduced a professional digital camera system.
In 1995, it launched the DC40 consumer digital camera.
And in 2001, it introduced the EasyShare system, designed to make digital photography easier for ordinary customers.
These were real products, backed by engineering and investment.
So the story isn't that Kodak refused to enter the digital market.
The story is that entering a market and building a profitable position within it are two very different achievements.
## 8
Kodak understood that consumers wanted simpler ways to take, store, share and print photographs.
Its EasyShare cameras and docking systems were designed around that experience.
The company also invested in digital printing and imaging technologies.
But digital cameras were becoming increasingly competitive.
Hardware prices faced pressure.
Technology improved quickly.
And customers gained more choices.
Kodak could participate in the transition without controlling the economics of the new industry.
The company that had dominated the film ecosystem was now competing in a very different market.
## 9
Digital photography changed more than the camera.
It changed what happened after someone pressed the shutter.
Photographs could be stored on computers, shared electronically and viewed without printing.
That weakened the relationship between taking pictures and buying film, chemicals or paper.
And as digital imaging spread, the traditional photographic supply chain came under pressure.
Kodak's challenge was not simply to sell a different kind of camera.
It was to replace an entire economic system that had supported the company for generations.
## 10
This is where technological leadership and business success begin to separate.
A company can invent important technology and still struggle to earn attractive returns from it.
Kodak had expertise, patents and a globally recognized brand.
But digital imaging demanded different capabilities, different investments and different sources of profit.
Meanwhile, the old film business was declining.
The transition required Kodak to fund new opportunities while managing the deterioration of established ones.
And eventually, the financial pressure became impossible to ignore.
## 11
By 2011, Kodak's financial statements showed a company under severe strain.
Annual revenue had fallen to approximately six billion dollars, down from roughly seven point two billion the previous year.
The company reported a substantial net loss.
Its filings described declining traditional-product volumes, competitive pressure in digital cameras and rising material costs.
This wasn't one product failing.
It was a business trying to finance a transformation while its historical sources of revenue weakened.
The numbers revealed how difficult that transformation had become.
## 12
On January nineteenth, 2012, Kodak and certain United States subsidiaries filed for Chapter Eleven bankruptcy protection.
The filing was not the disappearance of the company.
It was a legal restructuring process.
Kodak continued operating while attempting to reorganize its obligations and focus its remaining businesses.
But the symbolic impact was enormous.
A name that had once represented the future of photography was now restructuring in the middle of the digital age.
The company had helped create a technological revolution.
It had not successfully transformed its original business around it.
## 13
During restructuring, Kodak made difficult decisions about what would remain.
It moved away from much of its consumer digital-camera business.
It sold assets associated with online photo services and other operations.
It also reached agreements to sell digital imaging patents.
The company increasingly concentrated on commercial printing, packaging and imaging technologies.
These decisions reflected a different future from the consumer photography empire Kodak had once built.
The brand was still recognizable.
But the company behind it was changing.
## 14
On September third, 2013, Kodak emerged from Chapter Eleven as a reorganized company focused primarily on business imaging.
Kodak had not vanished.
It continued operating in commercial printing, film and related technologies.
Some consumer imaging activities and brand relationships continued through separate businesses and licensing arrangements.
The important distinction is that Kodak's bankruptcy did not mean photography itself had disappeared.
People were taking more digital photographs than ever.
What had changed was the economic structure of the industry, and Kodak's position within it.
## 15
So was Kodak's mistake inventing a technology that threatened its own business?
No.
Innovation wasn't the mistake.
The deeper failure was the inability to build a sufficiently strong and sustainable business as the market moved away from film.
Kodak recognized digital imaging.
It developed products.
It invested in technology.
But its historic advantages didn't translate automatically into the new industry.
That is the danger of disruption.
A company can understand where technology is going without successfully changing how it makes money.
## 16
Kodak's story isn't simply about a company that ignored the future.
It's about a company that helped create the future, participated in it, and still struggled to survive the economic transition.
Its old business model was extraordinarily successful.
Its new businesses couldn't replace that success quickly enough.
And that is the final lesson.
Inventing the next generation of technology does not guarantee that you'll own the next generation of business.
Sometimes the greatest threat to an empire isn't a competitor.
It's the disappearance of the economics that built it.
Every brand leaves evidence.
