# Facts and sources: Brand Coroner · Case 013 · New Coke

Editorial rule: every historical claim is supported by documentary evidence. Contemporary 1985 reporting and The Coca-Cola Company's own published history are preferred. Quotations on screen are typeset transcriptions labelled "not a facsimile".

**Main sources**

- **TIME, May 6, 1985**: John S. Demott, "Fiddling with the Real Thing" (4 pages, content.time.com).
- **TIME, June 24, 1985**: John S. Demott, "All Afizz Over the New Coke".
- **Company history**: The Coca-Cola Company, "New Coke: The Most Memorable Marketing Blunder Ever?", coca-colacompany.com, read Nov 2026.
- **History.com**: Christopher Klein, "Why Coca-Cola's 'New Coke' Flopped" (Apr 23, 2015; updated 2026).
- **CBS News**: Rachid Haoues, "30 years ago today, Coca-Cola made its worst mistake" (Apr 23, 2015).

| 03 | The Pepsi Challenge used blind taste tests; Coke's lead was slipping | TIME, May 6, 1985: "In 1972 it started the Pepsi Challenge, which pitted the two colas against each other in blind testing"; "Coke's share of the U.S. market last year slipped from 22.5% to 21.7%, while Pepsi's went to 18.8%". | 04 | Contemporary reporting: 55 to 45 for the new formula in blind comparisons | TIME, May 6, 1985: "The new Coke flavor beat the old one by 55% to 45%. When those same people were told what they were tasting, their preference for the new flavor was even more pronounced, 61% to 39%." |
| 05 | The research could not explain consumers' attachment | Company history: "What these tests didn't show, of course, was the bond consumers felt with their Coca-Cola" |
| 06 | Roberto Goizueta, chairman and chief executive, championed the change | TIME, May 6, 1985 names Goizueta as chairman and quotes the company calling the change "the most significant soft-drink development in the company's history". | 07 | Unveiled at New York's Lincoln Center; packaging updated | History.com: Goizueta introduced the formula at Lincoln Center on April 23, 1985, calling it "smoother, rounder, yet bolder—a more harmonious flavor". TIME, May 6, 1985: "white- and-red cans bearing the word NEW on a silver strip" |
| 08 | Complaints, stockpiling, protest groups, criticism in Atlanta | Company history: "A man in San Antonio, Texas, drove to a local bottler and bought $1,000 worth of Coca-Cola"; protest groups including "the Society for the Preservation of the Real Thing and Old Cola Drinkers of America"; "Protesters at a Coca-Cola event in downtown Atlanta in May carried signs with 'We want the real thing' ...". | 11, 12 | The decision to bring the original back; Keough on the research | CBS News (2015) quoting Keough at the July 11, 1985 press conference: research "could not measure or reveal the depth and abiding emotional attachment to original Coca-Cola felt by so many people". | 13 | Both sold for a time, with distinct advertising; renamed Coke II; gone from the U.S. | Company history: "Coca-Cola classic was sold alongside Coca-Cola ('new Coke'), and the two brands had distinct advertising campaigns" ("Catch the Wave", "Red, White and You"); "Later, the name of the new taste of Coca-Cola was changed to Coke II; the product is no longer available in the United States." ## Storyboard items not used

- **Logos as graphics.** No Coca-Cola, Coke or Pepsi logo is reproduced as a design element. Brand marks appear only where they are part of a photograph of a real object: a New Coke can, a New Coke bottle, an early-1980s Pepsi Challenge can and button, an 1890s poster, the 1915 bottle.
- **Invented quotes.**
  - **Goizueta, "A bolder, stronger Coca-Cola."** Replaced with the documented "smoother, rounder, yet bolder—a more harmonious flavor" (History.com).
  - **A Pepsi executive, "Coca-Cola just handed us an opportunity."** Replaced with Enrico's "The Real Thing is in real trouble" (TIME) and the ad line "the other guy just blinked" (History.com).
  - **The "Company abandons New Coke" newspaper clipping.** Not reproduced as a page.
- **Staged people.** No verified, freely licensed photographs exist of the April 23 press conference, of Goizueta or Keough, of 1985 protests or of the hotline room, so the film does not stage them: no woman with a sign, no switchboard operator, no executive at a podium. It uses a photograph of Lincoln Center and of Coca-Cola's Atlanta headquarters, and the quoted record.
- **Television footage, advertisements from 1985 and newspaper front pages.** These are copyrighted and are not reproduced. Their content is quoted on transcription cards with the source named.
- **A Coca-Cola Classic 1985 can and Coke II packaging.** No freely licensed photograph was found. Scene 13 shows the New Coke can and bottle and the company's own account of the two-brand period.
